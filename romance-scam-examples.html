Romance scam examples: recognize the pattern, not the story

Anonymized dating scam examples so you can spot the first money ask early. These are composites — not entertainment, and not one person’s file.

Date Safely editors Updated Sep 9, 2026 18+

Quick answer

Romance scam examples share a payment rail, not a plot. A match you have not met builds warmth, often moves you to WhatsApp, then asks for gift cards, a wire, a “bounced” check difference, or a crypto deposit. Amount is not the test. Stop sending. What these examples are for

People search romance scam examples and romance scam stories after a chat suddenly needs money — or after money already moved. This page is a handbook, not a highlight reel. Each block is a composite case pattern drawn from how the FTC and FBI describe romance fraud: no real names, no screenshots of victims, no “gotcha” ending.

A romance scam is a fake relationship used to take money. Also called romance fraud or a relationship scam. The types sit on the online dating scams map. You do not need a word-for-word match. You need the rail: irreversible payment from someone you have not met, after warmth, often after an off-app move.

How the first ask is usually worded: romance scam scripts. Walk-away list: romance scam red flags checklist.

Six common romance scam examples

Each pattern below follows the same four beats: how it started, the first money ask, the red flags, and what to do. The story can be swapped. The payment method usually cannot.

1. Military romance — distance as the cover story

How it started. A dating-app or Facebook profile in uniform. The job explains odd hours, “no FaceTime on base,” and why a visit keeps slipping. Daily check-ins feel loyal. Photos may be stolen from a real service member or generated.

First money ask. Leave papers, a replacement kit, a lawyer, or a ticket “so I can come to you.” Often gift cards or a wire “because my account is frozen overseas.” The number is framed as temporary.

Red flags. US service members and real contractors do not fund official process through a civilian’s iTunes or Target cards. Unscripted live video never happens. The visit date moves; the payment deadline does not. Isolation: “don’t tell anyone, it’s against regulations.”

What to do. Do not send. You do not have to disprove the deployment. One sentence is enough: you do not send money to people you have not met. Save the profile and the ask, then report.

2. Crypto “investment” after a dating-app meet

How it started. Weeks of warmth on Tinder, Bumble, or Instagram. They remember your dog. They talk about a future trip. Then a screenshot of a trading dashboard, or a “friend’s platform” that is not in the App Store you typed yourself.

First money ask. A small crypto “test” — $200 of Bitcoin, a deposit that even withdraws once. That success is the hook. Larger deposits and an unlock fee or “tax” come next. This path is pig butchering (dating-app arc: pig butchering on dating apps).

Red flags. You have not met. They onboard you to a brokerage from a chat. A withdrawal that “works” is inventory, not proof. Unlock fees never release earlier money.

What to do. Stop depositing. Do not pay the release fee. Call the bank and the crypto on-ramp the same day. File FTC ReportFraud and IC3. More money does not buy the earlier money back.

3. Gift-card emergency — “just this once”

How it started. Fast intimacy. They already talk like a couple. Then a crisis that only you can solve tonight: hospital deposit, medicine, rent, a child’s emergency. They already know which store sells the cards.

First money ask. Google Play, Apple, Target, or Steam cards. Sometimes they offer to pay you back next week. The amount looks small on purpose.

Red flags. Gift cards are cash that cannot be clawed back. Hospitals and landlords do not get paid in game credit. Urgency plus secrecy is the product. How the line is written: first money-ask scripts.

What to do. Do not buy the cards. Do not send a smaller amount as a compromise. If you already gave codes, tell the store and your bank the same day, then report the profile.

4. Fake cashier’s check or overpayment

How it started. A long-distance match wants to help with a bill, buy something through you, or send a “gift” ahead of a visit. A cashier’s check or money order arrives — or they promise one — for more than you discussed.

First money ask. Deposit it, keep a thank-you cut, and wire, Zelle, or gift-card the difference back — or buy equipment “for my work” and ship it. The check later bounces. You are the one who sent real money out.

Red flags. Banks can make funds look available before a counterfeit check clears. A romantic partner you have not met does not need you as their payroll. Pressure to move the extra before the check is fully verified is the tell.

What to do. Do not forward any of it. Take the instrument to your bank and say you suspect a romance overpayment scam. Do not “make them whole” on a second rail they control.

5. WhatsApp rush off the dating app

How it started. A match within hours: “the app keeps logging me out,” “WhatsApp is easier,” “Telegram is more private.” The dating app’s report button stays behind. This is the setup in most online dating scams — not a tech glitch.

First money ask. Sometimes the same day (a small favor). Sometimes after a week of voice notes. The rail is still gift cards, a ticket, Zelle, or a “try this platform” link.

Red flags. Off-app before live video or a real meet. They refuse an unscripted video on the original app. They punish you for wanting to stay where you can report. Isolation starts here.

What to do. Stay on the app until live video with a spontaneous ask. If they will not, end it. If they already asked for money, treat it as fraud and report the original profile while you still have it.

6. Lonely-widow (or widower) crypto companionship

How it started. Facebook, a widow/widower group, or a dating site aimed at older adults. Months of companionship after a loss. They are patient. They talk about faith, grandchildren, a visit that is always next month. Then “this is how I rebuilt after my spouse died” — a trading site, gold, or crypto.

First money ask. A modest deposit “so we can watch it grow together,” or help moving a pension into their platform. Later: a tax, unlock fee, or frozen account. Same pig-butchering steal, slower grooming.

Red flags. A new partner you have not met is managing your retirement money. They discourage you from telling adult children. Story details slip; the wallet instructions stay precise. A small profit shown on a dashboard is the hook.

What to do. Stop. Tell a trusted family member or friend the same day — shame is part of the design. Call the bank and any on-ramp. Do not pay a release fee. File FTC and IC3. The relationship was the pitch.

You do not owe them a debate about the hospital, the base, or the check.

Real people who want a relationship can handle “I don’t send money to people I haven’t met.” If that sentence ends the chat, the chat needed to end. Full list: romance scam red flags.

Shared pattern checklist

Use this as a walk-away list, not a quiz. One strong flag is a pause. Two or more means stop. You do not need every beat from the examples above.

  • You have not met in person (or you met once, briefly) and money is already the topic.
  • Rush off-app — WhatsApp, Telegram, iMessage — before live video.
  • Refuse unscripted live video, or only send clips and voice notes.
  • A crisis only irreversible payment solves — gift cards, wires, Zelle, crypto, a check difference.
  • Isolation — “don’t tell your family, they won’t understand us.”
  • A trading platform or unlock fee after weeks of warmth. That is pig butchering, not a couple investing.
  • Story details slip (job, city, the visit) while the money pitch stays smooth.

What to do now

Stop sending

No “one last” gift card. No unlock fee. No tax to release funds. No smaller amount as a compromise. More money does not buy the earlier money back.

Call the bank or card same day

Say romance scam or romance fraud — the wording helps the fraud team. Flag wires, Zelle, gift-card purchase points, cashier’s checks, and any crypto on-ramp. Speed matters more than perfect wording.

Report the profile and file with authorities

Block and report on the app. US: ReportFraud.ftc.gov and IC3.gov. UK: Action Fraud. Australia: Scamwatch / ReportCyber. Step-by-step: how to report a dating scam.

Save evidence, tell someone you trust

Screenshots of the profile, chat, wallet addresses, URLs, check images, and receipts. Shame is part of the design. A friend who already knows makes the next “please help again” script harder.

FAQ

Are these real romance scam stories?

No. They are anonymized composites of patterns the FTC and FBI describe — not one victim’s file and not entertainment. The useful part is the payment rail, not the plot. Hub: romance scams explained.

What is the most common romance scam example?

Warmth on a dating or social app, a move to WhatsApp or Telegram, then a small urgent ask (hospital, travel, gift cards) or a crypto “test.” Amount is not the test. First-ask language: romance scam scripts.

Is a small gift card still a romance scam?

If it comes from someone you met online and have not verified in person, treat it as one. Gift cards are irreversible on purpose. A small card is a test of whether the rail works.

What should I do if I recognize one of these patterns?

Stop. Do not pay a release fee. Call the bank or card the same day if anything already left, report the profile, then file FTC ReportFraud and IC3 in the US. Checklist: how to report a dating scam.
