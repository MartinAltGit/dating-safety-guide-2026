What is sextortion? Webcam blackmail, how it works, and what to do

A plain-language definition — not a scare page. What sextortion is, how webcam and chat blackmail usually unfold, how to keep evidence, and why the first rule is do not pay.

Date Safely editors Updated Sep 9, 2026 18+

Quick answer

Sextortion is online blackmail that uses intimate images, video, or the threat of a leak to demand money. It often starts in a dating chat or webcam session. Do not pay. Paying does not buy deletion. Save the chat, stop contact, then report the account and file with FTC ReportFraud, IC3, and local police. Action list: sextortion help.

Key takeaways

  • The leak threat is the lever. The steal is gift cards, crypto, or a wire.
  • Webcam blackmail is the same crime with a faster clock — record, threaten, demand.
  • Do not pay. A first payment almost always becomes a second demand.
  • Save evidence first, then block. File platform, FTC, IC3, and local police reports.

What sextortion is

Searchers type what is sextortion after a chat turns into a threat — or after a payment already left. The FBI’s language is plain: someone uses the threat of sharing intimate images or video to coerce you. In news and police reports you will also see webcam blackmail and online blackmail. Same crime, different labels. Action steps live on the sister page: how to deal with online blackmail.

A romance scam wants a fake relationship that ends in gift cards or a fake trading site. Sextortion skips the long courtship. The operator wants a file, or the appearance of a file, then an irreversible payment. Related hubs: romance scams explained and online dating scams.

Shame is part of the design. People freeze, then pay, then wait. Waiting is how a $200 gift card becomes a week of follow-up demands. The collage of your Instagram, workplace, or family names is usually scraped from public profiles — it is not proof they already posted anything. Ordinary video habits still help on dating apps: video chat safety.

How webcam and chat blackmail usually works

Most adult sextortion follows a short arc. You do not need every step. Plenty of people pay on the first threat because the screenshot already felt like the end of their reputation.

  1. Contact. Dating app, social DM, “wrong number,” or a random video chat. The profile can look ordinary. Photos may be stolen.
  2. Flirt, then a camera ask. They push for live video or intimate photos quickly. Some sessions look mutual until the recording starts.
  3. The threat. A screenshot, a clip, or a claim they recorded you. They list names from your public social accounts and say they will send the file unless you pay.
  4. The payment rail. Gift cards, Bitcoin or another crypto wallet, a wire, or an app transfer — chosen because it is hard to reverse.
  5. The second demand. If you pay, they come back. Later, a different account may offer to “wipe” the internet for a fee. That is a recovery scam — covered on the help page.

Off-app rushes, money asks, and refused live video are also online dating red flags. One strong flag is enough to stop. You do not owe them a debate about the story.

Webcam blackmail

Webcam blackmail is sextortion that starts on a live video session instead of a long text chat. The clock is faster. Someone appears on a dating video or a random cam chat, asks you to show yourself, records the window, then messages you — sometimes within minutes — with a demand.

Typical tells, without theatrics:

  • A sudden collage of your public accounts — username, workplace page, or family names pulled from search, not from a leak that already happened.
  • A countdown or “I already packed the emails” — urgency is the product. The goal is a same-day gift card or crypto send.
  • A request to move to WhatsApp or Telegram so the original site’s report button stays behind. Same move as other dating cons.
  • A claim that paying once deletes the file. It does not. The file, if it exists, stays with them.

Live video is still useful for spotting a fake dating profile — a spontaneous wave or turn is a different use than sending intimate video to a stranger. Habits: is video chat safe for dating. If the session already turned into a threat, skip the debate and go to evidence, then the help playbook.

You do not owe them a reply.

A real person who wants a relationship can handle “I stay on the app” and “I do not send intimate video to people I have not met.” If a threat is the next message, the chat needed to end. Save what you can see, then stop writing back.

Preserve evidence

Do this before you block, if the thread is still open. Deleting the chat to feel safer often deletes the only proof you have.

  • Screenshots of the profile, username, phone number, and the threat messages.
  • Any wallet address, gift-card codes they asked for, URLs, or payment app handles.
  • Dates, times, and the site or app where contact started.
  • A copy of emails or DMs in a folder you control — not only inside the app.

Do not send more images “to prove you will cooperate.” Do not open strange links they send after the threat. If money already left, keep the bank or crypto receipt. The report how-to has the same freeze-first habit: how to report a dating scam.

Do not pay

The FBI and consumer agencies are consistent: do not pay. Payment does not buy deletion. It tells the operator the threat works. A second ask — larger, same wallet or a new one — is the usual next step.

If you already paid, stop there. Do not send a “final” amount to keep them quiet. Call the bank, card, or crypto on-ramp the same day and say it was sextortion or online blackmail. Then file the reports below. Shame is why people wait; waiting is how the second steal happens.

Where to report

The platform

Use Report and Block on the dating app, social app, or video site. That is how the account leaves other people’s stacks. Blocking alone only hides them from you.

FTC and IC3 (US)

File at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and IC3.gov. The wording “sextortion” or “online blackmail” helps the complaint. UK: Action Fraud. Australia: Scamwatch / ReportCyber.

Local law enforcement

A local police report creates a record if images were posted, if you feel unsafe, or if a bank later asks for one. You do not need a perfect packet — take the screenshots you have.

Image-abuse tools, if you want them

Some adults use StopNCII.org to hash images so participating platforms can help block re-uploads. It is optional and not a substitute for IC3. Full order of operations: sextortion help.

If the person in the images is under 18, that is a different crime. Use the NCMEC CyberTipline and local police immediately. This handbook is written for adults on dating apps.

FAQ

What is sextortion?

Online blackmail that uses intimate images, video, or the threat of a leak to demand money or more content. It often starts in a dating chat or webcam session. Do not pay. Save evidence, stop contact, then report. Action list: sextortion help.

How does webcam blackmail work?

Someone starts a video or chat, records or claims to have recorded you, then threatens to send the file to friends, family, or work unless you pay. The collage of your social accounts is usually scraped. Same crime as sextortion; the contact method is live video. Habits that shrink ordinary dating-video risk: video chat safety.

Should I pay a sextortion demand?

No. Payment does not buy deletion. It confirms you will pay and usually brings a second demand. If money already left, call the bank or card the same day and still file FTC ReportFraud and IC3.

What should I do if someone is threatening to leak my photos?

Screenshot the profile, chat, usernames, and any wallet or gift-card ask. Then stop contact and block. Report the account. US: ReportFraud.ftc.gov, IC3.gov, and local police. Checklist: how to report a dating scam · sextortion help.
