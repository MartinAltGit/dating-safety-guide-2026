Scam phone numbers: check the digits. Do not call them back.

A calm workflow for a number that showed up after a dating match, a “wrong number” call, or a voicemail that already knows your name. Reverse lookup is a hint. Spoofing is normal. The useful moves are: do not call back, do not send a code, save the evidence, and report the rail.

Date Safely editors Updated Sep 9, 2026 18+

Quick answer

Do not call a suspected scam phone number back, and do not text it a one-time code. Screenshot the number, the thread, and any voicemail. Search the digits in quotes and treat people-search sites as incomplete — caller ID is routinely spoofed. If the number came from a dating match, stay on the original app, report the profile, then file with your carrier, FTC ReportFraud, and IC3 if money or threats are involved. Romance hub: romance scams explained. Report checklist: how to report a dating scam. Texts that started this: scam text messages.

Key takeaways

  • The number on the screen is not proof of who is calling. Spoofing is cheap.
  • A reverse lookup that “matches their name” is not a green light — and a blank result is not proof they are fake.
  • Never send a 2FA code, last-four of a card, or a selfie-plus-ID to someone who phoned you.
  • Dating context: stay on the app. The phone number is often the off-app door.

What not to do

People search scam phone numbers after a missed call, a voicemail, or a match who “just wants to hear your voice.” The useful first move is negative. Most of the damage happens in the callback.

  • Do not call the number back. A return call can confirm your line is live, connect you to a premium-rate trap, or put you on a script that asks for a “verification” code. Let unknown numbers go to voicemail. The FCC and FTC both treat callbacks to suspicious numbers as a risk, not a check.
  • Do not send a one-time code, PIN, or “last four.” Banks, Apple, Google, Cash App, and dating apps do not call you from a random mobile and ask you to read the SMS they just sent. That code is the key. Once it leaves, the account is theirs.
  • Do not “verify” with a selfie plus your driver’s license because a caller said they are app support, a bank, or the match’s roommate. That is identity theft, not safety.
  • Do not pay to unlock, trace, or “clear” the number. A second caller who offers to recover money or prove the first number was fake is usually the recovery scam. Guide: how to get money back from a scam.

If they needed a callback to prove they are real, they are not.

A real match can stay in the original dating-app thread and do unscripted live video. A real bank or carrier uses the number on the back of your card or the official app — not a missed call you return.

Reverse lookup — what it can and cannot tell you

Reverse lookup is useful as a hint. It is a poor verdict. Treat every result as incomplete.

  • Search the digits in quotes on a regular search engine. You may find a carrier spam thread, a forum complaint, or nothing. A pile of “scam” comments is a reason to stay offline — not a reason to call and confront them.
  • People-search and “who called me” sites recycle old listings, paid comments, and other people’s guesses. A first name that matches the dating profile is easy to fake. A business name on caller ID is easy to spoof.
  • A blank result is not a green light. New VoIP numbers, recycled mobiles, and numbers that have never been posted will look clean. AI photos fail reverse image search the same way. Live, unscripted video on the original app is still the dating check. Photo habits: how to spot fake profiles.
  • A hit that says “scam” is enough to walk away. You do not owe them a debate about a Google result.

Save what you searched and the screenshots. If you later file with the FTC or IC3, the trail matters more than a paid “full report” from a lookup site.

Spoofing and “local” numbers

Caller ID is a label, not a signature. Operators can display a bank, a local area code, or the same digits they just texted you. That is why a number that “matches” a text is not proof the text and the call came from one honest phone.

Common dating-adjacent patterns:

  • Neighbor spoofing — your area code, or the match’s claimed city, so you pick up.
  • A new mobile every few days — “my old phone died.” Disposable VoIP. The person did not move cities.
  • A “support” callback after you reported a profile. Real Tinder, Bumble, and Hinge safety teams do not cold-call members from a cell and ask for codes. App-side patterns: dating app scams.
  • A voicemail that already uses your first name — scraped from the dating app or a data leak, not intimacy.

If the call was a robocall or a spoofed bank/IRS script, that is a separate crime from romance fraud. Still do not call back. The report rails below still apply.

When it is romance or dating related

This page is the phone-number workflow. The broader crime — a fake relationship used to take money — lives on the romance hubs. Use this section when the digits showed up because of a match, a “wrong number” that turned warm, or a call that followed an in-app chat.

  • They want the number the same day. “The app keeps logging me out.” Dating apps log reports. The phone does not. Stay on the app until live video. Walk-away list: romance scam red flags.
  • The voice on the line does not match the photos, or they refuse video and offer a call instead. A voice note is not a check. Clips are not a check.
  • The number is the payment rail. They call to walk you to a gift-card aisle, a Bitcoin ATM, or a “verification fee.” First money-ask language: romance scam examples (scripts — not this page).
  • A “wrong number” call or text that becomes a relationship — same crew, different door. Channel guide: scam text messages. Types map: online dating scams.

If cash already left, stop sending and call the bank the same day. Do not pay a tracer who found you after the first loss. Recovery realities: get money back from a scam.

Report paths

Freeze the evidence

Screenshot the number, timestamps, the dating profile, the in-app chat, the call log, and any voicemail transcript. Copy wallet addresses and URLs into a note — do not open the link again. Do not send one last message to “catch them.”

Tell the carrier

On iPhone or Android, mark the caller as junk if the OS offers it. Tell Verizon, AT&T, T-Mobile, or your MVNO the number was used for a scam. Forward scam texts to 7726 (SPAM) in the US. Ask them to add the number to your block list. Carriers cannot “trace” a spoofed ID for you over chat support — they still want the report.

Report the dating profile

Block and report in Tinder, Bumble, Hinge, or Match so other people stop matching them. Blocking alone hides them from you and leaves them in circulation. Steps: how to report a dating scam.

File with authorities

US: ReportFraud.ftc.gov and IC3.gov. Include the number, the app username, and how you paid if money left. UK: Action Fraud. Australia: Scamwatch / ReportCyber. A single file will not get a refund by itself. It is how the pattern gets counted.

Anyone who calls you after you file and asks for a fee, a gift card, or a crypto “release” is not the FTC or the FBI. That is the second steal.

FAQ

How do I check if a phone number is a scam?

Do not call it. Screenshot it, search the digits in quotes, and treat lookup sites as incomplete. If it came from a dating match you have not met on live video, stay on the app and report the profile. Hub: romance scams.

Should I call a suspicious number back?

No. A callback can confirm your line is live or walk you into a code prompt. Let it hit voicemail. Real partners can video on the original app.

Can caller ID be faked?

Yes. Local area codes, bank names, and “their” mobile can all be spoofed. A reverse lookup that matches a name is not identity.

What if a dating match gave me this number?

Stay on the app. Do not send codes or ID photos. Report and block the profile, then file FTC ReportFraud and IC3 if money or threats are involved. App patterns: dating app scams.
